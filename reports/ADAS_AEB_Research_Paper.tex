\begin{abstract}
Pedestrian and Vulnerable Road User~(VRU) fatalities constitute a
critical global road safety challenge, with over 1.19~million deaths
annually~\cite{who2023}, of which pedestrians and cyclists account for
more than~26\%.
Automatic Emergency Braking~(AEB) systems are recognised as one of the
most effective countermeasures; however, contemporary implementations
rely on simplified kinematic stopping-distance models
($d = v^{2}/2\mu g$) that neglect tyre non-linearity, brake thermal
degradation, and driver state, resulting in a systematic underestimation
of stopping distance by~15--30\%.
This paper presents a physics-aware, monocular-vision-based AEB pipeline
that addresses these limitations through six tightly integrated modules:
(i)~real-time multi-class detection using a fine-tuned YOLOv8n model
on BDD100K achieving 78.3\%~mAP@0.5 for pedestrians and 77.1\% overall
across four object classes;
(ii)~SORT Kalman-filter tracking with persistent track identities;
(iii)~monocular depth estimation and Bird's-Eye View~(BEV) radar mapping
via the pinhole camera model;
(iv)~Pacejka Magic Formula longitudinal braking integrated at a 1\,ms
timestep with dynamic weight transfer and ABS modulation;
(v)~a novel brake rotor thermodynamics model---absent from all reviewed
commercial AEB systems---that continuously tracks rotor temperature
through the energy equation and Newton's cooling law, applying a
piecewise friction-fade penalty of up to~$+3.0$\,s to the AEB trigger
threshold; and
(vi)~a composite adaptive Time-to-Collision~(TTC) threshold combining
weather classification, driver drowsiness~(Eye Aspect Ratio via dlib),
and thermal state, spanning 1.5\,s under ideal conditions to 3.5\,s in
the worst case~(+133\%).
The system is validated against Euro~NCAP's four standardised pedestrian
crossing scenarios~(CPFA, CPNA, CPNC, CPLA) at speeds from 20 to
60\,km/h, achieving 100\% compliance at 20--30\,km/h.
Pedestrian injury severity is quantified using the Head Injury
Criterion~(HIC), demonstrating an 8-fold reduction---from HIC~2200
(AIS-5, Critical) to HIC~280 (AIS-2, Moderate)---when partial braking
reduces impact speed from 50 to 30\,km/h.
The complete pipeline runs at 15--20 frames per second on an NVIDIA
Tesla~T4 GPU, confirming real-time feasibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Road traffic collisions remain one of the leading causes of preventable
mortality worldwide.
The World Health Organization reports 1.19~million road deaths annually,
with pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcyclists---collectively termed
Vulnerable Road Users~(VRUs)---accounting for approximately~26\% of
global fatalities~\cite{who2023}.
In India alone, the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways recorded over
153,000 fatalities in 2022, with pedestrians constituting nearly 15\%
(approximately 23,400 deaths)~\cite{morth2023}.
The disproportionate exposure of VRUs to collision risk, combined with
their complete lack of structural protection, makes pedestrian-directed
AEB technology a high-priority safety intervention.

Contemporary production AEB systems---including those meeting Euro~NCAP
Level~2 requirements---determine braking urgency using the kinematic
model~\cite{seiniger2012}:
\begin{equation}
  d \;=\; \frac{v^{2}}{2\,\mu\,g},
  \label{eq:kinematic}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is a fixed nominal friction coefficient and $g$ is
gravitational acceleration.
This formulation embeds three critical assumptions that fail under
real-world conditions:
(a)~tyre force is proportional to normal load at all wheel-slip values,
ignoring the non-linear saturation described by the Pacejka Magic
Formula~\cite{pacejka1992};
(b)~brake friction coefficient is independent of rotor temperature,
neglecting the well-documented brake-fade phenomenon~\cite{day2014}; and
(c)~driver state---alertness, reaction time, situational
awareness---is invariant.
Our experiments confirm that these assumptions collectively lead to
underestimation of stopping distance by~15--30\%, which translates
directly to an AEB trigger that fires too late.

This paper addresses all three limitations in a unified software
pipeline that processes monocular video at real-time frame rates.
The system spans four coupled domains---computer vision, vehicle physics,
driver state estimation, and injury biomechanics---and is, to the best
of the authors' knowledge, the first pedestrian AEB framework to
simultaneously integrate:
(i)~a physics-calibrated tyre model with ABS modulation,
(ii)~a closed-loop brake thermodynamics model that adjusts the AEB
trigger as a function of rotor temperature, and
(iii)~a biomechanical injury severity prediction via the Head Injury
Criterion.

\subsection{Contributions}
The principal contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A real-time multi-class detection and tracking pipeline
        (YOLOv8n + SORT) achieving 78.3\%~mAP@0.5 for pedestrians and
        77.1\% overall across four VRU-relevant classes, coupled with
        monocular BEV radar projection.

  \item A five-force Pacejka-based longitudinal braking simulator at
        1\,ms timestep with dynamic weight transfer and ABS, validated
        within published reference corridors for six surface--speed
        combinations and showing 15--30\% accuracy improvement over
        kinematic models.

  \item A \textbf{novel} brake rotor thermodynamics model comprising a
        kinetic energy heating equation and Newton's cooling law that
        translates friction fade into a real-time AEB TTC penalty of up
        to~$+3.0$\,s---a capability absent from all reviewed commercial
        and academic AEB implementations.

  \item A composite adaptive TTC threshold controller combining weather
        classification~(ResNet-18), driver drowsiness~(dlib 68-landmark
        EAR), and brake thermal state into a single scalar threshold
        spanning 1.5\,s to~3.5\,s.

  \item Euro NCAP validation across four pedestrian crossing scenarios
        at five impact speeds with quantified HIC biomechanical injury
        outcomes, demonstrating an 8-fold severity reduction via partial
        braking.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Pedestrian Detection in ADAS}
Early pedestrian detectors relied on hand-crafted features such as HOG
with SVMs~\cite{dalal2005} and Deformable Parts Models~\cite{felz2010}.
Deep CNNs produced a step change: Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2017},
SSD~\cite{liu2016ssd}, and the YOLO family~\cite{redmon2018} progressively
improved the speed--accuracy trade-off.
Jocher~\etal~\cite{jocher2023} introduced YOLOv8 with a decoupled
anchor-free head and C2f backbone, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy
on COCO at real-time inference speeds.
We adopt YOLOv8n (nano) for its optimal throughput on embedded-grade
GPUs, fine-tuned on BDD100K~\cite{yu2020} for four VRU classes.

\subsection{Multi-Object Tracking}
SORT~\cite{bewley2016} combines Kalman prediction with Hungarian
assignment~\cite{kuhn1955} using IoU affinity.
DeepSORT~\cite{wojke2017} adds appearance re-identification at higher
computational cost; ByteTrack~\cite{zhang2022} improves low-score
detection utilisation.
For the latency budget of a real-time AEB pipeline, SORT's
sub-millisecond per-frame cost and deterministic behaviour are preferred.

\subsection{Monocular Depth Estimation}
Neural approaches such as MiDaS~\cite{ranftl2022} produce dense relative
depth maps but lack metric scale without additional calibration.
For VRUs with known anthropometric dimensions, the pinhole model provides
metrically accurate depth from a single bounding-box height measurement,
achieving depth errors below~10\% within the 30\,m AEB-relevant range.

\subsection{AEB Systems and Tyre Modelling}
Seiniger~\etal~\cite{seiniger2012} review AEB evaluation methodologies,
noting that the majority of systems use point-mass kinematic models.
Pacejka and Bakker~\cite{pacejka1992} established the Magic Formula tyre
model---a semi-empirical formulation capturing non-linear slip-force
behaviour across the full operating range.
Its application in AEB pipelines at video frame rates has not been
reported.

\subsection{Brake Thermodynamics and Fade}
Brake fade under sustained hard braking is well-characterised in
automotive engineering~\cite{day2014,tirovic2007}.
The energy deposited per stop follows kinetic energy conservation;
inter-stop cooling obeys Newton's law with velocity-dependent convective
coefficient.
Day~\cite{day2014} documents 30--70\% friction reduction above 450$^\circ$C
for semi-metallic pads.
Despite this, no reviewed AEB paper incorporates a thermodynamic fade
model as a real-time control input.

\subsection{Driver State Monitoring}
The Eye Aspect Ratio~(EAR)~\cite{soukupova2016}, computed from dlib's
68-point facial shape predictor~\cite{king2009}, provides a real-time
drowsiness indicator.
Previous works integrate driver state into warning systems~\cite{dua2013}
but do not propagate alertness into the AEB trigger threshold.

\subsection{Injury Biomechanics and Euro NCAP}
The Head Injury Criterion~(HIC)~\cite{versace1971} models head impact
severity; the Abbreviated Injury Scale~(AIS)~\cite{aaam2016} maps HIC
to a six-level score.
Euro NCAP's pedestrian AEB protocol~\cite{euroncap2022} defines four
standardised crossing scenarios.
Combined HIC and NCAP analysis in a single AEB framework has not been
reported in prior literature.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
% =============================================================================

The proposed system processes monocular video through a 14-step
sequential pipeline partitioned into two functional columns---a
\emph{Perception and Input} column (Steps~1--7) and a
\emph{Decision and Output} column (Steps~8--14).
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the complete data flow.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{fig_06_pipeline_flowchart}
  \caption{14-step per-frame AEB pipeline. The left column covers
           perception (Steps~1--7); the right column covers decision
           and output (Steps~8--14). An L-shaped connector routes
           processed tracks from Step~7 into the decision column.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

The Perception column performs: (1)~frame acquisition with optional
CLAHE enhancement; (2)~YOLOv8n inference; (3)~SORT tracking;
(4)~pinhole depth and BEV projection; (5)~lane detection and
ego-corridor polygon construction; (6)~spatial filtering; and
(7)~weather classification.
The Decision column performs: (8)~driver drowsiness assessment;
(9)~brake rotor temperature update; (10)~per-track TTC and adaptive
threshold evaluation; (11)~AEB trigger decision; (12)~Pacejka brake
integration; (13)~Euro NCAP scoring and HIC computation; and
(14)~BEV radar rendering and annotated output.

Two input modes are supported: pre-recorded video files and live
webcam streams.
Hardware requirements are a single forward-facing camera~($\ge$1080p
at 30\,fps) and an NVIDIA GPU for real-time YOLOv8n inference; all
physics modules run on CPU in under~1\,ms per frame.

\section{Perception Module}
\label{sec:perception}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

The BDD100K dataset~\cite{yu2020} provides 100,000 annotated driving
images across diverse conditions.
We curate a 6,000-image subset in a stratified 80:20 train--validation
split, selecting images containing at least one instance of the four
target classes: Pedestrian, Car, Bicycle, and Motorcycle.
Class imbalance (Car:Pedestrian ratio~$\approx$~3:1) is addressed
through stratified sampling.

Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation~(CLAHE)~\cite{zuiderveld1994}
is applied to each input frame with a tile size of $8\times8$ pixels
and clip limit~2.0, enhancing local contrast in low-illumination
regions (night-time, tunnels, underpass shadows) without amplifying
sensor noise.
Empirical evaluation confirms a 4--7\% improvement in detection recall
for small pedestrian instances in night-time frames.

\subsection{YOLOv8n Multi-Class Detection}

YOLOv8n employs a C2f backbone for feature extraction, a
Path Aggregation Network~(PAN) neck for multi-scale fusion, and an
anchor-free detection head that directly regresses bounding-box
coordinates and class probabilities~\cite{jocher2023}.
Pre-trained COCO weights initialise the network; fine-tuning runs for
30~epochs with SGD (momentum~0.937, weight decay~$5\!\times\!10^{-4}$),
cosine learning-rate annealing from 0.01 to 0.0001, and batch
size~16 on an NVIDIA Tesla~T4.
Training converges at epoch~22; early stopping prevents overfitting.
Inference uses confidence threshold~0.30 and NMS IoU~0.45.

Fig.~\ref{fig:detection} shows the mAP bar chart across all classes
and the training loss convergence over 30 epochs.
Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the normalised confusion matrix on the
validation set.
Fig.~\ref{fig:inference} shows a representative inference frame with
multi-class detections.

\subsection{SORT Multi-Object Tracking}

Persistent object identity is maintained by SORT~\cite{bewley2016},
which operates a bank of per-object Kalman filters with 7D state
$\mathbf{x} = [u,\,v,\,s,\,r,\,\dot{u},\,\dot{v},\,\dot{s}]^{\!\top}$,
where $(u,v)$ are bounding-box centre pixel coordinates, $s$ is box
scale~(area), and $r$ is aspect ratio.
Detection-to-track assignment uses the Hungarian
algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955} with IoU cost.
Track management: minimum 3-hit confirmation before AEB reporting;
maximum 1-missed-frame age before deletion.
Each confirmed track produces
$[x_1,\,y_1,\,x_2,\,y_2,\,\text{track\_id},\,\text{class\_id}]$
per frame.

\subsection{Ego-Corridor Filtering}

An ego-corridor polygon is constructed from detected lane markings
(Canny edge detection + Hough line transform) and tested for track
membership using Shapely's point-in-polygon predicate on each
bounding-box bottom-centre.
Only in-corridor tracks are forwarded to the TTC and AEB modules,
eliminating false activations from adjacent-lane and pavement objects.

\section{Depth Estimation and BEV Radar}
\label{sec:depth}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Monocular Depth via Pinhole Model}

For VRUs with known anthropometric dimensions, longitudinal depth~$Z$
is estimated from the bounding-box pixel height~$H_\text{px}$ using
the pinhole camera model:
\begin{equation}
  Z = \frac{f \cdot H_\text{real}}{H_\text{px}},
  \label{eq:depth}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the focal length in pixels and $H_\text{real}$ is the
known real-world height of the object class~\cite{pheasant2003}.
Lateral world coordinate~$X$ is:
\begin{equation}
  X = \frac{(c_x - c_{x_0})\cdot Z}{f},
  \label{eq:lateral}
\end{equation}
where $c_x$ is the bounding-box centre column and $c_{x_0}$ is the
image principal point.
Depth uncertainty analysis: for adult pedestrians~($H_\text{real} =
1.70$\,m, $\pm0.05$\,m inter-individual variation), the resulting
depth error is below~3\% at~10\,m and below~10\% at~30\,m---acceptable
for AEB TTC estimation at these ranges.

\subsection{Bird's-Eye View Radar Visualisation}

World coordinates~$(X,Z)$ are rendered in a real-time top-down BEV
radar panel alongside the annotated camera frame.
Three risk zones are colour-coded by instantaneous TTC:
\textcolor{green!60!black}{\textbf{green}} (SAFE, TTC~$>$~4\,s),
\textcolor{orange!80!black}{\textbf{yellow}} (WARNING, 2--4\,s), and
\textcolor{red}{\textbf{red}} (CRITICAL, TTC~$<$~2\,s).
Track history is visualised with fading dot trails.
Fig.~\ref{fig:bev} shows two frames from a live system run on BDD100K
dashcam footage, capturing the four-panel HMI display across a
3-second collision sequence.

\section{Physics-Based Longitudinal Braking Model}
\label{sec:dynamics}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Vehicle Parameters}

The simulation targets a representative compact sedan:
mass $m=1500$\,kg, wheelbase $L=2.70$\,m,
CoG height $h_\text{cg}=0.55$\,m,
frontal area $A=2.2$\,m$^2$, drag coefficient $C_d=0.30$,
rolling resistance $C_\text{rr}=0.015$.

\subsection{Pacejka Magic Formula}

The Pacejka Magic Formula~\cite{pacejka1992,pacejka2012} expresses
normalised tyre force as a function of longitudinal slip~$\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
  \mu(\lambda) = D\,\sin\!\bigl(C\arctan\bigl(B\lambda -
                E(B\lambda-\arctan B\lambda)\bigr)\bigr),
  \label{eq:magic}
\end{equation}
where $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$ are stiffness, shape, peak, and curvature
coefficients.
Longitudinal slip is:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda = \frac{v - v_\text{wheel}}{v},
  \label{eq:slip}
\end{equation}
where $v$ is vehicle speed and $v_\text{wheel}$ is peripheral wheel
speed.
Force per axle is $F_\text{tire}=\mu(\lambda)\cdot N_\text{axle}$.

\subsection{Five-Force Model and Weight Transfer}

Total longitudinal retarding force:
\begin{equation}
  F_\text{total} = F_\text{Pacejka} + F_\text{aero} +
                   F_\text{roll} + F_\text{grade},
  \label{eq:ftotal}
\end{equation}
with aerodynamic drag $F_\text{aero}=\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v^2$,
rolling resistance $F_\text{roll}=C_\text{rr}\,mg\cos\theta$,
and grade force $F_\text{grade}=mg\sin\theta$.
Dynamic axle load transfer during braking at deceleration~$a_x$:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta N_\text{axle} = \frac{m\,a_x\,h_\text{cg}}{L}.
  \label{eq:wt}
\end{equation}
The equations of motion are integrated at a 1\,ms Euler timestep.

\subsection{ABS Modulation}

ABS cycles brake pressure at~10\,Hz to maintain wheel slip near the
optimal $\lambda^*\!\approx\!0.10$--$0.20$ where $\mu(\lambda)$ peaks.
When $\lambda > 0.25$, pressure is reduced by 50\% for one 0.1\,s
cycle then restored.
This reduces stopping distance by~$\approx$25\% relative to
locked-wheel sliding~\cite{kiencke2005}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamics} shows the Pacejka model stopping distances
across five road surfaces compared to the kinematic estimate.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{fig_02_dynamics_stopping_distance}
  \caption{Stopping distance vs.\ road surface at 50\,km/h and
           100\,km/h.
           Pacejka\,+\,ABS results (teal) match published reference
           ranges on all surfaces.
           The kinematic model (implicit as reference lower bound)
           underestimates stopping distance by~15--30\%.}

\end{figure}

\section{Brake Rotor Thermodynamics Model}
\label{sec:thermo}
% =============================================================================

To the best of the authors' knowledge, no prior pedestrian AEB or
automotive safety publication has integrated a real-time rotor
temperature model into the AEB trigger logic.

\subsection{Thermal Heating Model}

During each braking event, the vehicle's kinetic energy is converted
to heat.
Temperature rise per stop:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta T = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_\text{veh}\,v^2}{m_r\,c_p},
  \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
where $m_r=40$\,kg is rotor mass and $c_p=450$\,J/(kg$\cdot$K) is
specific heat capacity of grey cast iron~\cite{day2014}.
For a single stop from 80\,km/h: $\Delta T \approx 82{^\circ}$C
per rotor, consistent with published single-stop measurements.

\subsection{Newton's Cooling Law}

Inter-stop cooling:
\begin{equation}
  T(t) = T_\text{env} + \Delta T\,e^{-k\,t},
  \label{eq:cool}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  k = k_\text{base} + k_\text{speed}\cdot v,
  \label{eq:k}
\end{equation}
with $k_\text{base}=10^{-4}$\,s$^{-1}$,
$k_\text{speed}=5\!\times\!10^{-5}$\,s$^{-1}$/(m/s),
$T_\text{env}=20{^\circ}$C.
At 80\,km/h cruise the cooling time constant is~$\approx$250\,s,
matching empirical disc decay data~\cite{tirovic2007}.

\subsection{Friction Fade Curve and AEB Penalty}

Brake friction multiplier~$\mu(T)$ from published semi-metallic pad
datasheets~\cite{day2014}:
\begin{equation}
  \mu(T) =
  \begin{cases}
    1.0 & T \le 300{^\circ}\text{C}\\
    1.0 - 0.3\tfrac{T-300}{150} & 300 < T \le 450{^\circ}\text{C}\\
    0.7 - 0.4\tfrac{T-450}{200} & 450 < T \le 650{^\circ}\text{C}\\
    \max(0.1,\;0.3-0.2\tfrac{T-650}{200}) & T > 650{^\circ}\text{C}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:fade}
\end{equation}

The corresponding AEB TTC penalty:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_\text{thermal} = \min\!\Bigl(3.0,\;\;
  0.5\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\mu(T)}-1\Bigr)\Bigr).
  \label{eq:penalty}
\end{equation}

panic stops (left) and the friction--penalty curves (right).

\section{Adaptive AEB Controller}
\label{sec:aeb}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{TTC Computation}

For each confirmed in-corridor track:
\begin{equation}
  \text{TTC} = d \;/\; v_\text{rel},
  \label{eq:ttc}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is depth from~\eqref{eq:depth} and $v_\text{rel}$ is the
relative approach speed.
The AEB engine operates on the minimum TTC across all confirmed
in-corridor tracks.

\subsection{Weather Classification}

A ResNet-18 classifier~\cite{he2016} fine-tuned on BDD100K weather
labels produces one of four scene classifications per frame:
Clear, Rain, Fog, and Night.
Weather-dependent AEB margin:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_\text{weather} =
  \begin{cases}
    0.0\,\text{s} & \text{Clear}\\
    0.3\,\text{s} & \text{Rain}\\
    0.5\,\text{s} & \text{Fog}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:delta_w}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Driver Drowsiness via Eye Aspect Ratio}

The EAR~\cite{soukupova2016} is computed from dlib's 68-point
predictor~\cite{king2009}:
\begin{equation}
  \text{EAR} = \frac{\|\mathbf{p}_2-\mathbf{p}_6\| +
                      \|\mathbf{p}_3-\mathbf{p}_5\|}
                     {2\,\|\mathbf{p}_1-\mathbf{p}_4\|},
  \label{eq:ear}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_1$--$\mathbf{p}_6$ are the six eye landmark
coordinates.
EAR~$<$~0.25 sustained for~$>$2\,s triggers the DROWSY flag:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_\text{driver} =
  \begin{cases}
    0.0\,\text{s} & \text{ALERT}\\
    0.5\,\text{s} & \text{DROWSY}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:delta_d}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Composite Adaptive Threshold}

\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\text{TTC}_\text{thresh} =
    1.5 + \delta_\text{weather} +
    \delta_\text{driver} + \delta_\text{thermal}}
  \label{eq:thresh}
\end{equation}
AEB activates when $\text{TTC} < \text{TTC}_\text{thresh}$.
Under ideal conditions: $\text{TTC}_\text{thresh}=1.5$\,s.
Worst case (fog + drowsy + faded): $1.5+0.5+0.5+1.0=3.5$\,s
($+133\%$).

A Random Forest classifier~\cite{breiman2001} trained on the feature
vector $[\text{TTC},\,d,\,v_\text{rel},\,\text{weather},\,
\text{EAR},\,T_\text{rotor}]$ provides a secondary AEB probability
estimate, achieving~91.4\% agreement with the physics-rule decision
on a held-out 15\% validation split.

\section{Euro NCAP Validation and Impact Biomechanics}
\label{sec:ncap}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Euro NCAP Pedestrian Crossing Scenarios}

Euro NCAP's AEB pedestrian protocol~\cite{euroncap2022} defines four
standardised scenarios tested at five impact speeds (20, 30, 40, 50,
60\,km/h), yielding a $4\times5=20$-combination matrix:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CPFA}: pedestrian emerges from the far side at 50\%
        overlap.
  \item \textbf{CPNA}: pedestrian crosses from the near side at 25\%
        overlap.
  \item \textbf{CPNC}: child emerges from behind a near-side parked
        car at 50\% overlap---the most restrictive scenario due to
        late detection~($\approx$20\,m).
  \item \textbf{CPLA}: pedestrian walks ahead in the ego lane~(same
        direction).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Head Injury Criterion}

When AEB cannot achieve full avoidance, residual impact speed
determines injury severity via HIC~\cite{versace1971}:
\begin{align}
  t_c      &= \pi\sqrt{m/k},           \label{eq:tc}\\
  a_\text{peak} &= \pi\,v_\text{impact}/(2\,t_c), \label{eq:apeak}\\
  \text{HIC} &= t_c\,(a_\text{peak}/g)^{2.5},     \label{eq:hic}
\end{align}
where $m=4.5$\,kg is pedestrian head mass, $k=35{,}000$\,N/m is
equivalent bonnet stiffness~\cite{untaroiu2013}, and $g=9.81$\,m/s$^2$.
AIS severity mapping~\cite{aaam2016}:
AIS~1~(Minor, HIC~$<$~150),
AIS~2~(Moderate, 150--500),
AIS~3~(Serious, 500--1000),
AIS~4~(Severe, 1000--1500),
AIS~5~(Critical, 1500--2500),
AIS~6~(Fatal, $>$2500).

\subsection{Brake Thermodynamics}

Six consecutive panic stops from 80\,km/h raise rotor temperature
to~428$^\circ$C (Table~\ref{tab:thermo}).

$\delta_\text{thermal}=0.19$\,s.
A kinematic AEB system is blind to this state and applies the
nominal~1.5\,s threshold, triggering~0.19\,s too late.
At $\ge$650$^\circ$C (extreme fade), the penalty caps at~3.0\,s.
Cooling to~$<$150$^\circ$C (safe operating range) requires
$\approx$10\,min at 80\,km/h cruise.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper presented a physics-aware, monocular-vision-based AEB system
for VRU protection that addresses fundamental limitations of kinematic
AEB implementations.
Six key conclusions emerge:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Physics Validated.} The Pacejka\,+\,ABS\,+\,weight
        transfer model matches published stopping distance references
        within~5\%.
        The kinematic model $d=v^2/2\mu g$ underestimates by~15--30\%---
        a systematic error unsafe for AEB calibration.

  \item \textbf{Brake Fade Captured~(Novel Contribution).}
        Six panic stops raise rotor temperature to~428$^\circ$C,
        reducing friction by~28\% and requiring~$+$0.19\,s additional
        AEB lead time.
        No prior AEB publication incorporates this effect.

  \item \textbf{Euro NCAP Compliance.}
        100\% pass rate at~20--30\,km/h across all four NCAP scenarios.
        CPNC~(child behind parked car) is the binding constraint at
        higher speeds, consistent with monocular camera detection range
        limits.

  \item \textbf{Adaptive AEB Threshold.}
        Composite TTC threshold scales~1.5--3.5\,s~($+$133\%) under
        worst-case fog, drowsiness, and thermal fade.

  \item \textbf{Injury Reduction~8$\times$.}
        Partial braking~50$\to$30\,km/h reduces HIC from~2200 to~280,
        lowering injury from AIS-5 Critical to AIS-2 Moderate.

  \item \textbf{Real-Time BEV Radar.}
        A monocular camera produces a top-down spatial awareness display
        at~15--20\,fps using pinhole depth projection.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Future Work}
Four directions are identified: (i)~stereo or short-range mmWave radar
fusion to extend CPNC detection range beyond~20\,m; (ii)~ResNet-18
weather classifier training on a balanced BDD100K subset targeting
$\ge$95\% accuracy; (iii)~NVIDIA Jetson Orin on-vehicle deployment
at 30\,fps in real mixed traffic; and (iv)~Kalman-filtered pedestrian
velocity estimation to replace the constant-speed TTC assumption.
